Born John Major, he was born on December 29, 1936, in Walthamstow, London, and educated at Harrow and Cambridge, where he studied law. Before his political career, he worked as a barrister and served in the Royal Air Force during the Vietnam War. He entered Parliament in 1970 and quickly rose through the ranks, becoming Home Secretary in 1979 and later Foreign Secretary in 1983.


John Major's premiership was characterized by his efforts to restore the Conservative Party'sin image following the divisive and unsuccessful leadership of Margaret Thatcher. He sought to rebuild the party's credibility and appeal, particularly after the defeat in the 1992 general election, which was a significant setback for the Conservatives. Major's government faced the immediate challenge of the European Community (now the European Union) and the Maastricht Treaty, which aimed to further integrate European countries. The treaty was controversial and faced opposition from various quarters, including the UK's own political parties and the public, leading to a referendum in 1992 where the majority voted against it. Major's government negotiated the opt-out for the UK, but the issue remained a contentious one throughout his tenure.


Despite this, Major focused on economic recovery, introducing the "Back to Basics" campaign to revitalize the economy and improve public services. The government implemented the "Right to Buy" scheme, allowing council house tenants to purchase their homes, which was both praised for increasing homeownership and criticized for contributing to the housing shortage. The Major government also faced the challenge of the "poll tax," which was unpopular and led to widespread protests and riots, particularly in Scotland and Wales. The unpopularity of the tax led to its eventual repeal.


In foreign policy, Major's government dealt with the Gulf War and the Balkans conflict, with the UK playing a significant role in the international coalition against Iraq. The government's handling of the conflict was seen as competent, but the peace process in the Balkans was criticized for being too slow and ineffective. The peace agreement in Bosnia was seen as a success, but the peace in Kosovo was less certain.


Domestically, Major's government faced the challenge of the "mad cow disease" outbreak, which led to a ban on British beef exports and had severe economic repercussions. The Conservatives also struggled with the issue of Northern Ireland, with the peace process being fragile and the Good Friday Agreement of 1998 being a significant milestone in the Northern Ireland peace process.


Major's premiership was also marked by the "Cash-for-Honours" scandal, where it was revealed that several members of his government had been offered peerages and honors in exchange for political support, leading to a major political scandal. This scandal damaged his party's reputation and contributed to the loss of the 1997 general election.


In 1995, Major's government was also dealing with the aftermath of the Black Wednesday, where the pound sterling was devalued to control inflation, and the UK's entry into the Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), which aimed to stabilize currencies within the European Monetary System. The ERM was a significant economic policy decision that had far-reaching consequences for the UK's economy.
