In 1995, the United Kingdom was under the leadership of John Major, who served as Prime Minister from 22 August 1990 to 1 May 1997. John Major, born on 26 September 1943, was a Conservative politician and the son of a coal miner. He was elected to the House of Commons in 1979, representing the constituency of Huntingdonshire. Major's tenure as Prime Minister was marked by significant economic challenges and political shifts.

One of the most notable challenges Major faced was the economic downturn of the early 1990s, which followed the sharp rise in interest rates in 1990 to combat inflation. This period, known as the "Lawson Boom," led to a significant increase in the cost of living and a rise in unemployment. Major's government struggled to manage the economy, and the resulting economic hardship contributed to the growing dissatisfaction among the public.

Despite these economic challenges, Major's government made some significant legislative achievements. One of the most notable was the introduction of the "Poll Tax," which replaced the previous system of local taxation. The Poll Tax, officially known as the Community Charge, was implemented in 1990 but was widely unpopular due to its regressive nature and the difficulty in paying it for many citizens. This tax became a major point of contention and a significant factor in the Conservative Party's electoral decline.

Another key area of focus during Major's tenure was the European Union. Major's government sought to maintain the UK's influence within the EU while also addressing concerns about sovereignty and economic integration. The Maastricht Treaty, which established the European Union, was a central issue during this period. Major's government negotiated the terms of the treaty, which included provisions for the UK to opt out of the Social Chapter, reflecting the government's desire to limit the EU's social and economic directives.

Internally, Major's government faced challenges related to party unity. The Conservative Party was divided, with tensions between those who supported a more centrist approach and those advocating for a return to Thatcherite policies. This internal strife, coupled with the economic difficulties, contributed to the erosion of Major's support.

In the 1997 general election, the Labour Party, led by Tony Blair, made significant gains, winning a landslide victory. This marked the end of Major's tenure as Prime Minister. Despite the challenges, Major's time in office was characterized by efforts to navigate the complexities of economic policy, EU relations, and internal party dynamics. His legacy is often seen as one of managing a period of significant economic and political change, which ultimately led to the transition of power to a new era of British politics.
